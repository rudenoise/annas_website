When AI and a changing world leave you overwhelmed

The world of work is changing faster than any of us were built for. Whether you're deep in it every day or watching it from the outside and fearing for your future, the anxiety it stirs up is real — and therapy can help you carry it.

Maybe you're a software engineer who spends all day building with, and racing to keep up with, AI — and you come home wired, drained, and unable to switch off. Or maybe you're not in tech at all, but you can feel the ground shifting beneath your job, your industry, your plans, and you're quietly afraid of what comes next. Either way, you're carrying something heavy that's hard to put into words.

When the people around you don't quite get it

One of the loneliest parts is the sense that no one really understands. If you work in tech, friends and family might picture a well-paid, enviable job and struggle to see why you're so frazzled — so you stop trying to explain the relentless pace, the pressure to constantly re-learn, the strange feeling of building the very thing that might reshape your own role.

And if you're outside the industry, you might feel the opposite: that you're "behind", that everyone else has it figured out, that admitting your fear about the future would be embarrassing. So you keep it to yourself, and it grows in the quiet.

Why this kind of anxiety hits so hard

It isn't in your head, and it isn't weakness. This particular anxiety has some very real ingredients:

  • Uncertainty with no off-switch. Our nervous systems cope badly with threats that are constant, vague and always in the news.
  • Pace. The expectation to keep learning and adapting endlessly, with no point at which you're allowed to feel "on top of it".
  • Identity. When your skills or your profession feel under threat, it can shake your sense of who you are and what you're for.
  • Isolation. Feeling that the people close to you can't see what you're dealing with.
  • Always on. Work, screens and worry blurring into evenings and weekends until there's no edge between them.
"You don't have to explain the technology here. We start with something simpler and more important — how it actually feels to be you right now."

How therapy can help

Therapy won't predict the future or tell you whether your fears will come true. What it can do is change your relationship with the anxiety, so it stops running the show. In our work together, that might look like:

  • A space where you don't have to translate. You don't need to justify why it's hard or teach me the tech — I'm interested in the human underneath it. We focus on how it feels, not how it works.
  • Separating fear from fact. Anxiety blends real, practical concerns with catastrophic "what ifs". Talking it through helps you see which is which, and where you actually have some ground to stand on.
  • Easing the physical load. The wired, can't-switch-off, tight-chested feeling is your body under sustained stress. We can work with that directly, so rest becomes possible again.
  • Reconnecting with what matters. When everything's about output and keeping up, it's easy to lose sight of your own values. Therapy helps you step back and ask what you actually want — from work, and from a life that's more than work.
  • Ending the isolation. Simply being properly heard by someone who takes it seriously can loosen a knot you've been carrying alone.

You can't slow the world down. But you can build a steadier, calmer place to stand inside it — and that's something we can work on together.
