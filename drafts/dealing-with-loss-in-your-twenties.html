Dealing with loss in your twenties

Grief is hard at any age. But losing someone in your twenties can carry a particular kind of loneliness — a sense of being out of step with everyone around you. If that's where you are, you're not alone, and it's not something you have to carry by yourself.

Your twenties are supposed to be the "best years of your life" — building a career, finding your people, working out who you are. So when loss lands in the middle of all that, it can feel like the ground has tilted while everyone else is still walking normally.

Why grief in your twenties can feel so isolating

Often it's the sense of being the only one. Most of your friends haven't lost a parent, a sibling, a partner or a close friend yet — so even when they care deeply, they may not know what to say, or they say the wrong thing, or they quietly drift because it frightens them. You can end up comforting them, or hiding your grief so you don't bring the mood down.

There's also the strange out-of-time feeling: scrolling past holidays and promotions and engagements while you're just trying to get through the day. Grief doesn't fit the story your twenties were "meant" to follow, and that gap can be painful all on its own.

There is no right way to grieve

One of the most freeing things to know is that grief has no correct shape or timetable. You might feel:

  • numb, or strangely fine, and then flattened without warning
  • guilty — for laughing, for coping, for things left unsaid
  • angry, at the world, at other people, or at the person you lost
  • exhausted, unfocused, or unlike yourself
  • behind your friends, as though everyone else moved on and you got stuck

All of this is grief. It doesn't mean you're doing it wrong or "not coping". And loss isn't only about death — the end of a relationship, an estrangement, a diagnosis, or losing the future you'd imagined can bring grief that's just as real, even if the world doesn't always recognise it.

"You don't have to be 'over it', explain yourself, or grieve on anyone else's timeline. You just have to be honest — and be met."

How person-centred counselling can help

Person-centred counselling offers something grief especially needs: a space that is entirely yours, where you can feel heard without having to protect anyone or perform being okay.

Rather than following a set programme or telling you which "stage" you should be in, I work at your pace and follow your lead. What that can offer is:

  • Somewhere to say the unsayable — the angry, guilty or frightening thoughts you can't share with friends or family.
  • To be genuinely understood, without judgement and without anyone rushing you towards feeling better.
  • No timeline and no pressure. Whether the loss was last month or years ago, grief is welcome here whenever it surfaces.
  • Room for all of it — the sadness and the numbness, but also the love, the memories, and slowly, who you are becoming alongside the loss.

Grief doesn't get "fixed", and therapy won't try to take it away. But being accompanied through it — properly heard, at your own pace — can ease the loneliness and help you carry it in a way that feels more bearable.
