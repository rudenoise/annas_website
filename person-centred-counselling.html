Understanding therapy

Person-centred counselling, explained

If you've come across the term "person-centred" and wondered what it actually means, here it is in plain English — the idea behind it, what a session feels like, and why it can help.

Person-centred counselling is a way of working that puts you at the centre — your feelings, your pace, and your own sense of what's right for you. Rather than being told what to do or what's "wrong" with you, you're given a genuine, accepting space to explore things and find your own way forward.

The one idea it all rests on

The approach was developed by the psychologist Carl Rogers in the mid-twentieth century, and it grew from a simple but radical belief: that people already carry within them the capacity to understand themselves and to grow — they just need the right conditions to do it.

In other words, you are the expert on your own life. My job isn't to diagnose you, analyse you, or hand you a set of instructions. It's to offer a relationship warm and safe enough that you can hear yourself think, feel what you feel, and move at your own pace.

The three things that make it work

Rogers described three qualities that, when they're genuinely present in the relationship, help people change. They sound simple, but together they're quietly powerful.

1. Being accepted, exactly as you are

Sometimes called unconditional positive regard. It means being met without judgement — no part of you is too much, too shameful, or too difficult to bring into the room. When we stop having to defend or hide parts of ourselves, we can finally look at them honestly.

2. Being truly understood

This is empathy — my genuine effort to see the world through your eyes and to understand how things feel from where you're sitting, rather than from the outside. Feeling deeply understood, often for the first time, is frequently where the shift begins.

3. A therapist who is real

Sometimes called congruence or genuineness. It simply means I show up as a real, honest human being rather than hiding behind a professional mask. That authenticity helps build the trust that makes the rest possible.

What a session actually feels like

People are sometimes surprised by how ordinary — and how gentle — it feels. There's no couch, no analysing your dreams, no being told what your problem "really" is. Instead, it's a conversation that you lead. You bring what's on your mind, and I help you explore it: reflecting things back, noticing what comes up, and staying alongside you as you make sense of it.

You won't be rushed, and you won't be pushed to talk about anything before you're ready. Some sessions feel like untangling a specific problem; others are simply a space to think out loud and be heard.

"You are the expert on your own life. Therapy just gives you the space and the company to hear yourself clearly."

What it can help with

Because it works with the whole person rather than a single symptom, person-centred counselling can help with a wide range of experiences — among them:

  • anxiety, worry and feeling overwhelmed
  • low mood and depression
  • low self-worth and confidence
  • relationship difficulties
  • grief and loss
  • navigating change, or simply feeling stuck

You don't need a specific diagnosis or a "big enough" reason to come. If something feels hard, that's reason enough.

How it's different from more directive approaches

Some therapies are structured around techniques, worksheets or set programmes. Person-centred counselling is more relational — the healing happens through the quality of the relationship itself, and through being met with acceptance, understanding and honesty. Neither is "better"; they simply suit different people and different moments. Many people find the person-centred way especially freeing precisely because it isn't about being fixed or corrected.
